\cvsection{Summary}
Regular CVs list what went right. This one lists what went sideways, what I missed, and what I learned along the way. Those are the parts that actually shaped how I work. I am a freelance Cloud Architect and Software Engineer with 19+ years of hands-on experience (12+ of them freelance). I am currently back at Union Investment as IT architect for three teams of the funds squad. In parallel I run BitterQuant, a private ML research project on systematic trading. So far it has taught me that the simulator matters more than the model.

Along the way I accepted a leadership role I was not ready for. I chose the familiar search engine over the better one and built a middleware that did not need to exist. I have also spent years being skeptical of Kubernetes while running software on it. Today coding agents write most of my code under my supervision. The agent takes over tests and documentation; the architecture, the go/no-go and the blame stay with me. Even with the best models it is still ``garbage in, garbage out''.

\cvsection{Key Impacts (of Things Not Going to Plan)}
\begin{cvkpis}
\kpi{1}{leadership role taken on too early}
\kpi{8 yrs}{for a Diplom, thanks to a day job}
\kpi{1,065}{hidden type errors in one monorepo}
\kpi{2}{trading approaches killed by realism}
\kpi{34\%}{reported coverage that was really 87\%}
\end{cvkpis}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Promoted to Head of Development without management experience (2008):} The company and I both assumed technical competence would turn into leadership. It did not, at least not automatically. I underestimated the human factor, learned through training and reflection, and settled on a laissez-faire style that suited both me and the team.
  \item \textbf{Chose Sphinx over ElasticSearch because it was familiar:} ElasticSearch's features seemed exotic at the time and later became the industry standard. The platform migrated to ElasticSearch anyway, while I was back as a backend developer. Years later, at Union Investment, I evaluated ElasticSearch against Algolia properly before choosing.
  \item \textbf{Built a middleware with 100\% uptime that should not have existed:} In hindsight, an integrated solution with eventing on AWS Lambda would have been cheaper, simpler, more robust and faster. It was very reliable, though.
  \item \textbf{Met microservices in real life and did not like what I saw:} Simple changes needed modifications across many services, and the architecture was not open to change. Since then I have preferred Self-contained Systems.
  \item \textbf{Growth through deliberately skipping analysis:} I went straight to Web Components after a validated PoC instead of evaluating every integration path. That saved about 60 person-days. Sometimes the responsible analysis is knowing when to stop analysing.
  \item \textbf{Growth through feedback:} 360\textdegree{} surveys rated my technical competence highly. They also asked for slower, more accessible explanations for less technical stakeholders and more openness to critical feedback. I am working on both.
\end{itemize}

\cvsection{Recent Roles: What Went Sideways, and What Grew}

\cvrole{IT Architect \& Platform Engineer \textbar{} Union Investment}{Apr 2026 \textendash{} present}{Freelance, part-time, via percision services GmbH \textbar{} architect for three teams, hands-on in Team Rechner (public financial calculators)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The bot that quietly stopped working:} Renovate had been intermittently broken for months. Getting it running again cut the median time-to-merge of dependency PRs from \textasciitilde2\,h to 13\,min. The cheapest tech debt to pay off is the automation nobody noticed was off.
  \item \textbf{The bug was in the thermometer:} Reported backend line coverage was 34\%. Generated code and non-Quarkus tests were miscounted; fixing the measurement showed the real figure, 87\%.
  \item \textbf{Making CI more honest:} I cut automatic Playwright retries against deployed sites from 3 to 0, so flaky tests surface instead of hiding. In a sibling team's monorepo, a new type-check gate uncovered 1,065 hidden type errors, all now fixed.
  \item \textbf{Handovers have a price:} Here, operations is handled by separate teams. Every handover costs communication effort and brings together different working styles and assumptions. This confirmed my conviction in ``you build it, you run it''.
\end{itemize}

\cvrole{Cloud Architect \textbar{} LBBW}{Nov 2024 \textendash{} Dec 2025}{Freelance, remote \textbar{} ``Phoenix'' IT modernization (\textasciitilde80 people) \textbar{} architectural responsibility for 10 of 30 applications}

  \item \textbf{No template fits all:} I cataloged hundreds of legacy applications (Spring Boot, Tomcat, IIS, Oracle, MS-SQL) using the R-methodology. Diverse stacks meant individual migration approaches, even with standardized templates.

\cvrole{Software Lead \& Cloud Architect \textbar{} Union Investment}{Oct 2021 \textendash{} Dec 2024}{Freelance, remote \textbar{} B2B portal InvestmentWelt \textbar{} team of 5\textendash{}7, technical Product Owner \textbar{} not the lead of the overall modernization}

\cvsection{Lessons That Keep Coming Back}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Familiar is not the same as right:} Sphinx taught me this in 2008. A careful ElasticSearch vs. Algolia evaluation applied it fifteen years later.
  \item \textbf{The straightforward approach often wins:} Pub/Sub on Event Hubs beat ``impossible'' search designs. Eventing on Lambda would have beaten my own middleware.
  \item \textbf{Paying down tech debt pays off:} Most of my measured results in 2026 come from paying down debt, and the whole team profits.
  \item \textbf{An autonomous, motivated team is gold:} Confirmed once again at every engagement where I had one.
  \item \textbf{Agents do not replace judgement:} Coding agents multiplied my throughput, but engineering best practices still apply. A codebase needs a good developer and agent experience to stay maintainable.
\end{itemize}
